VIDEO 1 (PART 1)

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 1: how models get attacked

Description:

A machine-learning model adds three things an attacker can go after that classic security does not cover: its training data, the model file itself and the answers it gives. In this first video of the series we go through those attacks using the vocabulary of NIST's taxonomy (NIST AI 100-2e2025), and watch them work in two small demos on synthetic data.

What we cover:

Why a model needs its own security, and where each attack lands in the life of a model.
NIST's three attacker objectives (availability, integrity and privacy), what the attacker can touch and how much they know.
Evasion: by misreporting three fields on a form, 78.7% of people scored high risk get a low-risk score. Measuring smoking and weight in clinic instead of taking them from the form brings it down to 20.7%.
Poisoning: 2% of poisoned rows plant a backdoor that turns every high-risk score into low risk, while holdout accuracy barely moves.
Privacy attacks: membership inference, attribute inference, reconstruction, property inference and extraction.
Extraction: 200 queries are enough for a copy that agrees with the model on 86.9% of cases.
Supply chain: four files tampered with on the way to the user that pass the usual check, and how a pinned hash refuses them.
Generative AI and prompt injection, direct and indirect.
Real cases from MITRE ATLAS, from Tay to EchoLeak.
JADEPUFFER (2026): ransomware run by an AI agent that got in through an exposed agent server, encrypted and deleted production databases, and that nothing limited in what it could do.
Why every defence has a cost or a gap.

All the data is synthetic: nothing in the demos is a real patient. The repository has the demos, the technical guide and the presenter guide, in English and Spanish:

VIDEO 2

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 2: NIST, MITRE ATLAS, OWASP, the EU AI Act and the UK Code

Description:

In the first video we saw how models get attacked. In this one we look at what the five most cited frameworks say about those attacks, and how to make one test speak all of their languages.

The frameworks do different jobs. NIST and MITRE ATLAS describe: the taxonomy and vocabulary, and the attacker's tactics with real cases. OWASP prioritises: two top-ten lists for the people who build models and LLM applications. The EU AI Act and the UK Code of Practice require. GRAIMATTER, the case in video 3, is a different kind of document: a detailed control framework for one branch, privacy, in one setting.

What we cover:

NIST AI 100-2e2025 and MITRE ATLAS (release 2026.09, with 16 tactics and 73 case studies): describing the attacks.
The OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10, still a 2023 draft, and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025: prioritising them, and which demo in the repository shows each item on the machine-learning list.
EU AI Act, Article 15: resisting data and model poisoning, evasion and confidentiality attacks is a legal duty for high-risk systems. The Digital Omnibus, in force since 27 July 2026, moves those duties to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (regulated products). And EDPB Opinion 28/2024: a model trained on personal data is not anonymous by default, which is exactly where GRAIMATTER starts.
UK: the Code of Practice for the Cyber Security of AI, 13 principles across five stages of the lifecycle, which became the European standard ETSI EN 304 223. Four of its provisions match the attacks in video 1: threat models, limits on queries, hashes of model components, and outputs that must not let anyone rebuild the model or its data. And the NCSC and CISA guidelines that came before it.
One attack, five names: a crosswalk that ties the five together, shows which demo runs each attack, and is what the release gate in video 4b uses.

This is a summary of the documents, not legal advice.

VIDEO 3

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 3: GRAIMATTER, when the output is the model

Description:

A trusted research environment (TRE) lets approved researchers work with sensitive data, such as health records, without taking it away. For years it has checked tables and statistics before letting them out. Now the output is a trained model, and a model can carry the people it was trained on. The GRAIMATTER green paper (DARE UK, 2022) answers one specific question: when is a trained model safe to release?

In this video we look at GRAIMATTER as a high-trust case of adversarial machine learning: one sector that has worked out one branch, privacy, in depth.

What we cover:

Why the attacker that matters is white box: once the file is released, whoever has it has everything.
Three ways a model file carries people: nearest neighbours, SVCs and deep decision trees.
The three risk types: the researcher who saves too much by accident, the one who hides data on purpose (we see it work in demo 6, in video 4b), and the outside attacker.
The two families of controls: check the file before it leaves (model disclosure controls), then limit its queries (model query controls).
The paper's five sets of recommendations: technical, ethics and legal, costing, training and public involvement.
Six habits that carry over to any high-trust deployment, and what GRAIMATTER does not cover: evasion, poisoning and generative AI.

The demos and the attacks in detail come in videos 4a and 4b.

VIDEO 4A

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 4a: the attacks in detail, the CIA triad and the supply chain

Description:

In the first video we went over the attacks quickly. In this one we go through them one by one, class by class of NIST's taxonomy, and run each demo section by section to see where every number comes from.

What we cover:

The CIA triad in machine learning, and how to read a NISTAML identifier.
Availability: data and model poisoning, clean-label poisoning and energy-latency attacks.
Integrity: evasion, black-box evasion, backdoors, targeted poisoning, clean-label backdoors and model poisoning.
Demo 3, live. Evasion: 78.7% of high-risk people evade by misreporting a form, and measuring smoking and weight in clinic brings it down to 20.7%. Backdoor: with 2% poisoned rows the trigger works every time, accuracy barely moves, and an outlier screen finds only 25% of the quiet poison.
Confidentiality: membership inference, attribute inference and extraction, and three families of defence.
Demo 5, live. A model that memorised its training data gives away who was in it: the attacker is right 92% of the time, where a coin toss gets 50%, and guesses a hidden field (smoking) for 94% of members against 60% of strangers. Returning only a label still leaks; stopping the model from memorising closes most of the gap and costs no accuracy.
Supply chain: a swapped model with a backdoor, code hidden in a pickle, a key cut from 256 to 40 bits and a safe-wrapper rule lowered from 20 rows to 1.
Demo 4, live: all four files pass the usual check, and a SHA-256 pinned at approval refuses every one. We also see its limit: a hash taken from the tampered copy accepts it.

VIDEO 4B

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 4b: privacy attacks in detail, and a release gate

Description:

The second half of the detailed walkthrough: privacy attacks, which is GRAIMATTER's ground. We run demos 1 and 2 section by section, see how data can be hidden inside a model with demo 6, and finish with a release gate that judges the results of every demo against the EU AI Act, the UK Code, NIST and GRAIMATTER.

What we cover:

Privacy according to NIST: extraction, reconstruction, membership inference, property inference and attribute inference.
Extraction, live (demo 3): 200 queries give a copy that agrees with the model on 86.9% of cases.
How to read AUC, advantage, TPR at 10% FPR and FDIF, and why how rare a member is changes what a flag means.
Demo 1, GRAIMATTER's checks written by hand, in the order of its report, section 1 to section 8: the rows the researcher never sees; a planted person whose two hidden fields come back after 20 queries; what epsilon means; an unconstrained tree with a membership AUC of 0.708 that is refused; the same tree through a safe wrapper, AUC 0.528, that is released; the quiet failure an average can hide; models that store rows; edits after fit(); and a query budget that counts rows, not calls. Every number on screen is worked out from its counts, for example AUC 0.708 = (35,038 + 18,387 / 2) / 62,500.
Demo 2 with the official SACRO-ML package: why the same tree gives AUC 0.708 under one attack and 0.500 under another.
Demo 6, hiding data in a model: 111 bytes in the low bits of a network's weights with accuracy unchanged, and a message hidden in the answers to a few trigger inputs. An accuracy check passes both. The TRE's answer is to transform every released model (clip bits, quantise, add noise or train with differential privacy), not to try to spot what is hidden.
Generative AI: the classes NIST adds, the JADEPUFFER case from video 1, and where LLMs and agents get tested, in safety-trust-engine.
The release gate: GRAIMATTER passes, so does the supply chain, and Article 15(5) of the EU AI Act fails on evasion, extraction and the backdoor. A control whose demo did not run comes out as not evidenced: never a silent green.
What this repository does not decide, and how to run the demos and the gate yourself.

VIDEO 4C

Suggested title: Adversarial machine learning, part 4c: defences in practice, securing an agent's database access

Description:

JADEPUFFER (video 1) won because nothing limited what the agent could do. This closing video is the other side: a real MCP (Model Context Protocol) server that lets an LLM agent query a database and nothing more, built so that every door that attack walked through is closed, and closed in layers so no single trick opens it. When you connect an agent (Copilot Studio, ChatGPT, Claude) to a production database, the agent is the untrusted, over-eager operator from JADEPUFFER, and the question from the frameworks videos — what can this agent do, with what credentials, and what needs a human to say yes? — gets answered in running code. All secrets, the real domain and tenant identifiers are removed; the example is a read-only server in front of PostgreSQL.

What we cover:

The setup: an agent that never touches the database, speaking to an MCP server behind a reverse proxy that exposes a small, read-only toolset.
Door by door, each JADEPUFFER move against the control that closes it: an exposed endpoint against TLS plus OAuth 2.0; app-as-root against a least-privilege read-only user with explicit DENY; arbitrary SQL against a single-SELECT filter and a per-query timeout; unwatched activity against rate limits and blocked-query logging; secrets on the box against a secrets vault.
The three layers, and which one really holds: database permissions are the hard boundary; the per-tenant access policy is filtering, not the boundary; and prompt or knowledge guidance is advisory only. Telling an agent to behave is not a control.
The honest limits: the real read-only guarantee is the database user's permissions, not a keyword filter; edge filtering by IP or domain is unreliable, so OAuth is the primary control, with mTLS or a Zero-Trust proxy stronger.
Demo 7, live: the same design in miniature, in SQLite. An agent reads a patient note that tells it to drop a table, wipe the appointments and export the password hashes, and it sends ten attacks. Tested alone, a keyword filter stops 5 of 10, an access policy 2 of 10 and the prompt guidance none; only the database permissions stop all ten, and the three that slip past both text checks (a comma join, a write disguised as a read, a query that never ends) are exactly the ones that would have done harm.
The checklist to answer before any agent ships: what tools, with what credentials, what is irreversible and needs a human, is it bounded and watched, and do you enforce or just ask.

This maps to OWASP LLM06 (excessive agency) answered in the affirmative, the ETSI EN 304 223 and UK Code provisions on API rate-limiting and least privilege, and the GRAIMATTER habit of testing against the strongest attacker you can imagine.
